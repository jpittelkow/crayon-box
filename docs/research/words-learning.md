# Research: what helps 3 to 5 year olds start writing words, and what good tools do

Research for the last item of section 5 of the [to-do list](../todo.md) ("tracing short words"), done 2026-11-15
on the owner's request ("What would be good to help kids learn? What is out there that is known good tools we can
build?"). The decision it feeds is [ADR 0039](../adr/0039-writing-words.md) (Proposed). It builds on
[letters-learning.md](letters-learning.md) and does not repeat it: fingers first, fading help, the hint ladder,
praise and short spoken lines all carry over to words.

**How sure each finding is**, as in the letters research: **strong** (several studies or a large review),
**moderate** (one or two decent studies), **weak** (thin, older kids only, or guidance without studies). Sources
marked _(abstract)_ were checked from an abstract, an index page or a summary, not read in full.

**The honest headline.** Almost all the good evidence on writing and spelling words is from 5 and 6 year olds in
their first year of school. The kids here are 4 and under. For them the well-supported steps are: their own name,
hearing the sounds letters make, and seeing that a word is letters in a row from left to right. Sounding out a
word alone is a skill of 5 to 6. So words in the app are something a kid can always finish with no reading at all,
and the sounds ride along for when they are ready.

---

## 1. How writing words is learned

### 1.1 The kid's own name comes first (moderate to strong; studies are links, not trials)
- Among 60 Dutch children aged 4 to 5½, 65 % could write their name readably. Those children used **the first
  letter of their name** for its sound when writing other words (in 5.2 of 8 words that held it, against 2.6 of 8
  that didn't), and heard that letter's sound in spoken words better than other sounds. Children who couldn't write
  their name yet did neither. The authors call the name's first letter "among the first letters that are written
  phonetically". One session, no control group, a small sample.
  [Both-de Vries & Bus 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2815302)
- Children know the name of their own first initial better than other letters and print it better; in that study
  the advantage did not reach the letter's sound.
  [Treiman & Broderick 1998](https://sites.wustl.edu/treiman/files/2020/02/Treiman-Broderick-1998-Whats-in-a-name.pdf)
  _(abstract)_
- Children write their own name at a higher level than any other word, in two languages.
  [Levin, Both-de Vries, Aram & Bus 2005](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/writing-starts-with-own-name-writing-from-scribbling-to-conventional-spelling-in-israeli-and-dutch-children/F3BB6F301AA826FDCD1BC6973FBA5151)
  _(abstract)_
- Writing one's name in preschool is one of the strongest early signs of later reading (letters research 1.6, 1.7).

**For the app:** the first word every kid meets is their own name, taken from the profile a parent typed. Its first
letter gets the most attention (its name and its sound). A name is also the one word a 3 year old cares about.

### 1.2 What kids can do with sounds, by age (moderate for the order, weak for the ages)
- The usual order is bigger pieces first: words, then **syllables** and **rhyme** (about 3 to 4), then the **first
  sound** of a word (about 5), then pulling a three-sound word apart or pushing it together (about 5½ to 6).
  Charts differ by half a year or more and are teaching guides, not norms.
  [Victoria early-years literacy toolkit](https://www.vic.gov.au/literacy-teaching-toolkit-early-childhood/learning-foci-emergent-literacy/phonological-awareness),
  [Keys to Literacy teaching sequence](https://keystoliteracy.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Phonological-Awareness-Teaching-Sequence.pdf)
  _(abstract)_
- Hearing single sounds mostly appears **once reading is being taught**, not before it, and the order is not the
  same in every language. [Duncan et al. 2006](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ777516) _(abstract)_

**For the app:** nothing a kid must do to finish a word may depend on hearing or blending sounds. A 3 year old
traces the letters and hears the word; a kid near 5 starts to notice that "sun" begins with "sss". No quiz.

### 1.3 Sounds taught together with letters beat sounds alone (strong, ages 5 to 6)
- The US National Reading Panel's review found teaching children to pull words apart and push them together works
  about twice as well **when the letters are in front of them** (effect on reading about 0.67 against 0.38, on
  spelling 0.61 against 0.34), and better when it sticks to **one or two skills** than when it mixes many.
  Figures as quoted by summaries of the report, not checked in the report itself.
  [Reading Rockets on the panel's findings](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/phonological-and-phonemic-awareness/articles/phonemic-awareness-instruction),
  [the panel's chapter](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/sites/default/files/publications/pubs/nrp/Documents/ch2-I.pdf)
  _(abstract)_
- 90 kindergartners were randomly given sound practice plus letter sounds, letter sounds alone, or nothing. Only
  the first group read and spelled better afterwards.
  [Ball & Blachman 1991](https://www.readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/does-phoneme-awareness-training-kindergarten-make-difference-early-word)
  _(abstract)_

**For the app:** every letter in a word can say its sound when touched, and the app does exactly two things with
sounds: says them one by one, and joins them into the word. No rhyming games, no "which one doesn't belong".

### 1.4 A box for each sound (moderate; small studies)
- "Sound boxes" (Elkonin boxes): a row of empty boxes, one per sound; the child fills a box for each sound, later
  with a letter. In a Head Start study every child got better at pulling words apart and stayed better after the
  prompts went. In first grade they did no better than another activity (sorting words).
  [Understood.org](https://www.understood.org/en/articles/evidence-based-literacy-strategy-elkonin-sound-boxes),
  [Head Start study, Durst & Joseph](https://journals.charlotte.edu/dialog/article/view/499/616) _(abstract)_

**For the app:** show the word as a row of empty boxes that fill from the left as each letter is written. It costs
almost nothing and shows three things without words: how many letters, which one is next, and that writing runs
left to right. In three-letter words one box is one sound.

### 1.5 Say the word stretched, not chopped (moderate; one small trial)
- 38 kindergartners who knew letter sounds but couldn't yet read were taught to blend either with a stop between
  sounds ("sss … uuu … nnn") or without one ("sssuuunnn"). **Without stops worked better**, and it carried over to
  words with sounds that can't be stretched (b, t, k).
  [Gonzalez-Frey & Ehri 2021](https://www.readingrockets.org/research-by-topic/connected-phonation-more-effective-segmented-phonation-teaching-beginning-readers)
  _(abstract)_

**For the app:** when a word is finished the narrator says it slowly as one stretched sound, then normally. The
first words on the path should start with a sound that can be held (s, m, f, n, r, l): sun, sat, map, mud, run,
red. Whether an AI voice can say a stretched word well is unknown; it needs takes picked by ear.

### 1.6 Writing a word "your own way" (moderate, kindergarten)
- Kindergartners trained to write words by their sounds, with a gentle nudge one step ahead of where they were,
  learned to read more new words than groups trained on sounds alone or on drawing.
  [Ouellette & Sénéchal 2008, via the authors' lab](https://carleton.ca/cllr/wp-content/uploads/sites/172/2025/11/parentbooklet2009.pdf)
  _(abstract)_
- A randomized trial with kindergartners who were weak at hearing sounds found the same direction; it passed the
  US What Works Clearinghouse review "with reservations".
  [Sénéchal, Ouellette, Pagan & Lever 2012](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/82934) _(abstract)_
- Some summaries call a later study (2017) proof of cause; it followed children over time and shows a link.

**For the app:** a real next step, but for kids who already know most letter sounds: 5 and up. It also needs a
person or a clever checker to give the nudge. Keep it as a later level; make its narrator lines now.

### 1.7 By hand, with letter tiles, or typing (mixed)
- For learning single letters, writing by hand beats typing (letters research 1.1).
- For learning how words are spelled, second graders learned as much by typing as by printing; a first-grade study
  favoured handwriting.
  [Ouellette & Tims 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3923165),
  Cunningham & Stanovich 1990 (as described in that paper)
- Montessori classrooms have children build words from loose letters (the "moveable alphabet") before they can
  write them. It is a century of practice with no controlled trial behind it.
  [Montessori Public](https://www.montessoripublic.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/MP_Articles-PDF_V71-Zoll_Saylor_Feinberg.pdf)
  _(abstract)_

**For the app:** both tracing a word and building it from letter tiles are reasonable; neither is proven better.
Tiles suit a kid whose hand isn't ready for small letters; tracing keeps the hand learning the shapes. No
keyboard (ADR 0003 blocks it anyway).

### 1.8 Pictures: they give the word its meaning, and can pull eyes off the letters (mixed)
- A picture beside a word can stop a child looking at the letters, because the picture already gives the answer.
  The effect is clearest in small studies of children with learning disabilities, and reviews disagree.
  [Singh & Solman 1990](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1286268)
- A different use of pictures does help: a letter drawn **inside** a picture of something that starts with its sound
  (an s as a snake). 36 five year olds learned 9 of 10 letter sounds better that way and still knew them a week
  later. [Shmidman & Ehri 2010](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/embedded-picture-mnemonics-to-learn-letters/811797128148942848-9814)
  _(abstract)_

**For the app:** a kid who can't read needs the picture to know what word this is, so each built-in word has one.
It shows when the word opens and comes back big at the end; while a letter is being traced the screen holds only
the letter. Letters drawn as pictures are a later idea for teaching sounds (26 drawings).

### 1.9 Capitals or small letters (weak; guidance)
- Capitals are easier to form and known first (letters research 1.2). The best-known US handwriting program starts
  names in capitals and aims for "title case" (a capital, then small letters) before school.
  [Learning Without Tears](https://www.lwtears.com/node/1502) _(abstract)_
- Books print words in small letters, so sooner or later words are small letters.

**For the app:** follow what the kid already traces: capitals while only the capitals set is on; once a parent
turns on small letters, names become a capital plus small letters and words become small letters.

## 2. Do apps like this help at all?

- **Across 36 trials** of learning apps for preschool to grade 3, children gained about a third of a standard
  deviation, more in preschool than later and more for narrow skills (letters, sounds) than broad ones (strong;
  apps differ a lot). [Kim et al. 2021](https://reacheveryreader.gse.harvard.edu/?p=2498) _(abstract)_
- **48 children aged 2 to 5** used letter matching, tracing and drawing apps 30 minutes a week for 9 weeks. Against
  a waiting group they gained in letter names and sounds, print concepts and name writing; **not** in writing
  letters (moderate). [Neumann 2018](https://researchportal.scu.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Using-tablets-and-apps-to-enhance/991013006098002368)
  _(abstract)_
- **About 50 four and five year olds** from low-income homes used one free learning app at home for 10 weeks and
  moved from the 34th to the 47th percentile on an early-literacy test, against children using other apps
  (moderate; small, and reported loudest by the app's maker).
  [UMass Amherst on Arnold et al. 2021](https://www.umass.edu/news/article/new-educational-app-shows-promise)
- **A research-built letter-sound game** tested in over 20 languages gives mixed results; it helps most with 6 and 7
  year olds who are behind (moderate).
  [Frontiers in Education 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2020.00132/pdf)
  _(abstract)_

**For the app:** expect a modest, real gain in knowing letters and their sounds, and in name writing. Don't expect
the app to teach handwriting on paper by itself (letters research 2.1), and keep telling parents that sitting
beside the youngest is what helps most (letters research 5.1).

## 3. Known good tools, and what each one does

Named here only to point at how they work. The app takes the mechanics, never a name, a character, art or a
sound (ADR 0011). App features are from store pages and reviews, checked 2026-11-15; none of these apps has its own
published trial that was found, except where section 2 says so.

| What the kid does | Who does it well | Evidence behind the idea | For us |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Traces a word one letter after another**, hears each letter, then the word | *Writing Wizard* (L'Escapadou), highly rated by reviewers | Tracing as a first step (letters 1.1) | The core of ADR 0039; our tracing screen already does a letter |
| **A parent adds their own words** and records each one | *Writing Wizard* (a paid add-on; reviewers single out how much a parent can customize) | Own name and family names matter most (1.1) | Family words, kept on the device; the narrator can't say a name, a parent's recording can |
| **Tap the start dots, then trace, then write with no guide** | *LetterSchool*; *Duolingo ABC* has "guardrail" then "freehand" | Help that fades; free writing teaches most (letters 1.1, 2.3) | Already planned for letters (plan phase F); words follow it |
| **Builds a word from loose letters** in a row of boxes; a letter says its sound when touched; three-letter words first | *Montessori Crosswords* (L'Escapadou); the Montessori moveable alphabet; classroom sound boxes | Sounds with letters (1.3), sound boxes (1.4) | A second activity, "Build it", with big tiles and no wrong answer |
| **Drags letters into a word's outline** while each one says its sound, then a short cartoon shows what the word means | *Endless Alphabet* (described from general knowledge; the reviews found don't spell the mechanics out) | Sounds with letters (1.3); meaning from a picture (1.8) | The same idea as Build it; the picture at the end is our small version of the cartoon |
| **Letter-sound matching against the clock**, made by reading researchers | *GraphoGame* | Its own trials, mixed (section 2) | Not for us: timing and right-or-wrong don't suit 4 and under (ADR 0003, 0012) |
| **Name cards, sound boxes, loose letters** on a table | Preschool and kindergarten classrooms | 1.1, 1.3, 1.4 | The parent area can suggest them: a name card on the fridge does more than any screen |

**What the good ones share:** one word at a time; huge letters; every letter makes its sound when touched; no
wrong-answer buzzer; one celebration per word; a picture or a little scene that gives the word a meaning; a parent
can add the family's own words.

**What to leave out:** an on-screen keyboard, timers, scores, spelling tests, word lists to memorise by sight,
sentences, and anything a kid has to read.

## 4. What the evidence does not settle
- Whether tracing a word teaches a child under 5 anything about that word beyond its letters. No study was found.
- Tracing a word against building it from tiles, at any age.
- How many repeats of a word are useful, and whether the same few words or many different ones are better.
- Whether an AI voice can stretch a word ("sssuuunnn") well enough to help.
- Whether a kid of 3 will stay with a word of three letters to the end. That is for the playtest.

## 5. In short, for the ADR
1. **Own name first**, with most care on its first letter.
2. **Then short words**, one huge letter at a time, in a row of boxes that fills from the left.
3. **Every letter can say its sound**; a finished word is said stretched, then normally.
4. **A picture gives each word its meaning**, shown before and after, not during.
5. **Nothing depends on reading or blending**; no wrong answers.
6. **Family words** a parent adds and records.
7. **Later:** build a word from letter tiles; fade the guide; write it your own way (5 and up).
8. **Any narrator line these need must be made before 2026-12-31** (ADR 0037).
